Thomas Robert Malthus & the Principle of Population

1766–1834 · An Essay on the Principle of Population, first published 1798

Does market exchange harmonize our interests, or conceal a conflict between them? The question we ask of every writer. Malthus's answer is the darkest one we will meet: the deepest conflict is not between classes or nations, but between human numbers and the earth that has to feed them — and no arrangement of society can talk it away.

The world that produced it

Why anyone wrote this in 1798

Malthus wrote to win an argument he was losing at his own dinner table.

His father, Daniel Malthus, was an admirer of the radical optimists of the age — the Englishman William Godwin and the Frenchman the Marquis de Condorcet. In the wake of the French Revolution, both men argued that human society was perfectible: that reason, better institutions, and the end of private property would one day abolish poverty, war, and inequality, and set humanity on a path of endless improvement.

The younger Malthus — a Cambridge-trained mathematician and an ordained clergyman of the Church of England — thought this was a beautiful fantasy, and he set out to show why it could never happen. Not because people are too selfish or institutions too corrupt, but because of a fact of arithmetic he believed no reform could escape.

The timing mattered. England in the 1790s was crowded with the things that made the argument feel urgent: a population visibly growing, the enclosure of common land, the first stirrings of the Industrial Revolution, food shortages and high grain prices, and a system of Poor Laws that taxed parishes to give relief to the poor. To Malthus, that relief was not kindness — it was fuel on the fire.

  1. 1793Godwin's Enquiry Concerning Political Justice argues society is perfectible.
  2. 1795Condorcet's Sketch predicts indefinite human progress. The Speenhamland system begins topping up poor wages.
  3. 1798Malthus answers, anonymously, with the Essay on the Principle of Population.
  4. 1803A much larger 2nd edition, now under his name, adds a way out: "moral restraint."
  5. 1834The New Poor Law is passed, shaped by Malthusian arguments. Malthus dies the same year.

The argument in his own words

Two ratios that never meet

Malthus starts from two things he treats as fixed facts about human beings: people must eat, and "the passion between the sexes" will continue roughly as it is. From these he draws the engine of the whole book.

"Population, when unchecked, increases in a geometrical ratio. Subsistence increases only in an arithmetical ratio."

— Malthus, Essay on the Principle of Population (1798), ch. 1

A geometric ratio multiplies: 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32… Population, left alone, doubles about every 25 years. An arithmetic ratio merely adds: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5… Food production, even improving steadily, can only be pushed up in slow, additive steps because land is limited. A multiplying line always, eventually, leaves an adding line far behind — no matter how you start them.

If population always tends to outrun food, then something must forever be holding it down to the level the food can support. Malthus calls these forces checks, and sorts them into two kinds.

  • Positive checks — they raise the death rate.Famine, disease, war, and the plain "misery" of the poor. These are nature's crude way of matching numbers to food, and they fall hardest on those at the bottom.
  • Preventive checks — they lower the birth rate.In 1798 he lists mainly "vice." In the 1803 edition he adds moral restraint — delaying marriage until you can support a family — the one humane escape he allows, because it lowers births without misery or vice.

"The power of population is indefinitely greater than the power in the earth to produce subsistence for man."

— Malthus, Essay (1798), ch. 1

The trap, and the case against the Poor Laws

Put the ratios and the checks together and you get what economic historians later called the Malthusian trap. Suppose wages rise. Families can afford more children, more survive, the population grows — and the extra mouths bid up the price of food and bid down wages until people are back at bare subsistence. Any gain for the mass of people is temporary; the population principle claws it back.

This is why Malthus opposed the English Poor Laws. Relief money, he argued, let poor families have more children than their own labor could feed. That raised the total population and the demand for food without raising the food supply, so it pushed up prices and spread the same fixed amount of bread more thinly — making the poor as a whole worse off while seeming to help them. It is a genuinely uncomfortable argument, and he meant it to be.

Where he lands on the course question

Conflict, but not the kind you expected

Adam Smith had offered a hopeful picture: individuals pursuing their own gain are led, "as if by an invisible hand," to outcomes that serve everyone. Godwin went further — perfect harmony was coming. Malthus stands against both, but from an angle all his own.

For Malthus the fundamental conflict is not employer against worker, or nation against nation. It is humanity against the soil — the multiplying drive of population against the stubborn limits of the land. That conflict is written into nature, not into any political system, so no revolution, no redistribution, no perfect set of institutions can dissolve it. Rearrange society however you like; within two generations the population principle refills it with the same scarcity and the same misery.

So Malthus's answer to the course question is bleak: markets don't so much harmonize our interests or hide a conflict between us as run up against a conflict none of us chose — between how fast we can breed and how fast the earth can feed. Progress, for the mass of people, is a door that keeps swinging shut.

The modern counterpart — and what gets lost

"Malthusian" today

The word outlived the man. Today "Malthusian" is shorthand for any warning that growth will crash into a natural ceiling — Paul Ehrlich's Population Bomb (1968), the Limits to Growth report (1972), debates about carrying capacity, food, water, and climate. Economic historians also use the "Malthusian trap" precisely: for almost all of human history before about 1800, living standards really did hover near subsistence, exactly as Malthus described.

What lines up

On the whole pre-industrial past, he was roughly right. Income gains were eaten by population; famine and disease really were the regulators. And the core math — a constant growth rate eventually overtakes a fixed additive one — is simply true.

What gets lost

He missed two things that broke the trap almost as he wrote: sustained technological growth in farming (food grew geometrically too), and the demographic transition — as people got richer, they chose to have fewer children, the opposite of his assumption.

There is a subtler loss too. Reduced to a slogan about overpopulation, Malthus stops being what he was: a clergyman working out how a good God could allow permanent human misery, and answering that scarcity is what goads mankind out of idleness into industry. The modern "doomer" caricature keeps the gloom and throws away the theology and the moral argument that gave it its shape.

The strongest case for him

Before you decide he was just wrong

It is easy, from 2026, to file Malthus under "famously mistaken" — we are eight billion people and better fed than ever. But the honest version of his argument is harder to dismiss than that.

For roughly the entire span of human history up to his own lifetime, Malthus was describing reality accurately: wages returned to subsistence, and population was checked by hunger and disease. He had no way to know that the one exception in all of history — sustained, compounding technological growth — was beginning in the mills a few miles from where he wrote. His mistake was not bad logic; it was standing at the single hinge point in history where his own law stopped applying. And the underlying warning — that a finite planet cannot support unlimited exponential growth forever — is exactly the argument environmental scientists still make today. To answer Malthus, you cannot just point and laugh at the population bomb that didn't go off; you have to explain why the escape happened, and whether it can last.
