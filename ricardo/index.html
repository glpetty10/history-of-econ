David Ricardo & the Division of the Spoils

1772–1823 · On the Principles of Political Economy and Taxation, first published 1817

Does market exchange harmonize our interests, or conceal a conflict between them? Ricardo is the first writer we meet who answers both at once. Between nations, his theory of comparative advantage finds a harmony so deep that even a country worse at making everything still gains from trade. But inside a nation, his theory of rent and distribution uncovers a standing conflict over how the harvest is split — one where the landlord's gain is everyone else's loss.

The world that produced it

A stockbroker takes on the landlords

Ricardo is the one great classical economist who got rich first and theorized later.

Born in London to a family of Sephardic Jewish stock-dealers, he went to work on the exchange at 14, made an enormous fortune trading government bonds — famously profiting on the news from Waterloo in 1815 — and retired young to the life of a country gentleman. He came to economics as an amateur, reportedly after picking up Adam Smith's Wealth of Nations on holiday, and he worked out his system in letters and pamphlets with his close friend and sparring partner Thomas Malthus, whom you met in Build 1.

The burning issue of his day was the Corn Laws — tariffs that kept cheap foreign grain out of Britain and so kept the price of "corn" (meaning grain generally) high. After the Napoleonic Wars, landlords wanted that protection to continue; manufacturers and workers wanted cheap bread. Ricardo threw himself into the fight on the side of free trade, and in doing so built the two ideas he is remembered for: a theory of rent that explained why the landlord's interest is opposed to everyone else's, and a theory of comparative advantage that proved Britain would be richer importing its grain and specializing in what it did best.

He even bought his way into Parliament, sitting as an MP from 1819 until his early death in 1823, and arguing these cases on the floor of the Commons. Where Smith wrote as a philosopher of the whole social order, Ricardo wrote as an engineer of abstractions — stripping the economy down to a few classes and a few hard rules, and following the logic wherever it led, even when it led somewhere grim.

The big idea · international harmony

Comparative advantage: why the worse country still wins

Here is Ricardo's most surprising and durable result. Smith had already argued that two parties should each specialize in what they do best and trade. The puzzle Ricardo cracked was harder: what if one country is better at making everything? Shouldn't it just make everything itself and trade with no one?

No — and the reason is the single most counter-intuitive idea in classical economics. What matters is not whether you are better in absolute terms, but what each good costs you in the other good you could have made instead — its opportunity cost. Trade according to that, and both sides gain.

Ricardo's own example uses England and Portugal, cloth and wine. Portugal can make both with less labor — it has the absolute advantage in each. Watch what happens anyway:

Labor-hours to make 1 unitClothWineCost of 1 cloth, in wine
England1001200.83 wine
Portugal90801.13 wine

Portugal is better at both (90 < 100 cloth, 80 < 120 wine). But look at the last column — the opportunity cost. To make one cloth, England gives up only 0.83 units of wine; Portugal gives up 1.13. So cloth is cheaper for England to make, even though England is slower at it, because England's wine is so much worse that not-making-wine barely costs it anything. The mirror holds for Portugal: giving up cloth to make wine costs it less than it costs England.

The rule: England should pour its labor into cloth (its comparative advantage), Portugal into wine (its comparative advantage), and the two should trade. The total of cloth-and-wine in the world goes up, and — at any exchange rate between the two countries' internal costs — both countries end up with more of both goods than if each tried to make everything alone. The less productive country gains too. Nobody has to lose for trade to help.

This is pure harmony of interests — and it is the engine of the game attached to this build. There you play England, decide how to use your workers, and bargain a trade with Portugal; you will find you can only reach prosperity by doing the thing that feels wrong: making no wine at all and trusting the exchange.

The dark idea · conflict at home

Rent, and why the landlord is everyone's opponent

If comparative advantage is Ricardo's sunny side, his theory of rent is his shadow. It is the idea that made him say something no polite economist of his day wanted to say out loud: that one whole class of society grows rich precisely by making everyone else poorer.

Start with a fact about land: it is not all equally good. Some fields are fertile, some are stony. When a country is small and its population low, only the best land is farmed, and food is cheap. But as population grows (here Ricardo borrows Malthus), more food is needed, so farmers are pushed onto worse and worse land — and onto the old land worked harder for smaller extra returns. This is diminishing returns in agriculture.

Now the twist. The price of grain has to be high enough to make it worth farming the worst land in use — the marginal land that barely pays. But that same high price is paid for grain grown on the best land, which produces far more for the same effort. The difference between what the good land yields and what the marginal land yields does not go to the farmer or the worker. It is skimmed off by the person who owns the good land, as rent.

"Rent is that portion of the produce of the earth which is paid to the landlord for the use of the original and indestructible powers of the soil."

— Ricardo, Principles (1817), ch. 2

So rent is not a cause of high grain prices — it is a result of them. And it grows relentlessly as population rises and worse land is tilled. Here is the chain that follows, and it is the heart of Ricardo's gloom:

  • Population grows → worse land farmed → grain price rises.Diminishing returns force society down the ladder of land quality to feed more people.
  • Rising grain price → rising rent.The gap between the best land and the marginal land widens, and landlords pocket the whole difference, having done nothing.
  • Rising grain price → rising subsistence wage.Bread costs more, so the wage that merely keeps a worker alive must rise too — but only to subsistence, never above (the "iron law" he shares with Malthus).
  • Higher rent + higher wages → the capitalist's profit is squeezed.What's left for the person who actually invests and employs shrinks from both ends.

This is why Ricardo makes the claim that defines his politics: the landlord's interest is permanently opposed to the interest of every other class. The worker lives at subsistence either way. The capitalist, whose profit drives all investment and growth, watches it drain away into rent. Only the landlord — contributing nothing but ownership of a fixed natural resource — gets steadily richer as the nation struggles.

The system in one picture

Distribution is the whole game

Smith had asked what makes a nation's wealth grow. Ricardo shifted the question to how the wealth is divided, and announced that this, not growth, was the real subject of the science.

"To determine the laws which regulate this distribution is the principal problem in Political Economy."

— Ricardo, Principles (1817), Preface

He pictured the whole national product — one great harvest — split three ways among three classes, each with its own law:

🌾 Landlords → rent

Receive the surplus of the good land over the marginal land. Their share rises automatically as population grows and farming spreads to poorer soil. They earn it by owning, not working.

👷 Workers → wages

Receive the subsistence wage — just enough to live and reproduce. Fixed in real terms by the iron law of wages; it buys the same bread no matter how rich the nation gets.

🏭 Capitalists → profit

Receive what is left after rent and wages. Because the first two tend to rise, profit is the residual that gets squeezed — and profit is the fund out of which all growth is financed.

⏳ The destination → the stationary state

As rent climbs and profit falls toward zero, the capitalist has nothing left to reinvest. Accumulation halts, growth stops, and the economy freezes in a rich-landlord, poor-everyone-else equilibrium.

Notice how different this is from Malthus's single lever. Malthus had one conflict: numbers against the soil. Ricardo has a three-way tug-of-war, and he can show mathematically that over time the rope is pulled toward the landlord — which is exactly why repealing the Corn Laws mattered so much to him. Letting in cheap foreign grain would stop the march onto bad domestic land, hold down rent, hold down the subsistence wage, and keep profit alive so the economy could keep growing instead of ossifying.

Underneath all of this sits his labor theory of value: the exchange-value of a good is governed chiefly by the quantity of labor needed to produce it. Ricardo held this more rigorously than Smith, wrestled honestly with the awkward cases (goods that need lots of machinery or time), and never fully resolved them — but he handed the labor theory of value, nearly intact, to the writer who would turn it into dynamite: Karl Marx, who you meet in Build 5.

Where he lands on the course question

Harmony abroad, conflict at home

Ricardo's answer to our running question is a genuine split-screen, and that is what makes him pivotal.

Between nations, markets harmonize. Comparative advantage is one of the strongest "invisible hand" results ever proven: let countries specialize and trade freely and everyone gains, even the country that is worse at everything. Here Ricardo out-Smiths Smith — the harmony is deeper and more surprising than Smith himself showed.

Within a nation, markets conceal a conflict. The very same free-market logic, applied to land and population, produces the theory of rent — and rent is a zero-sum machine. What the landlord gains in rising rent, the capitalist loses in falling profit. The harmony of exchange sits on top of a structural war over the division of the product, and Ricardo was the first to put that war in equations.

So Ricardo's answer is: both, and they are linked. Free exchange across borders really does harmonize interests — which is exactly why he fought the Corn Laws. But the distribution of what's produced at home hides a permanent conflict between landlords and everyone else. He is the hinge of the whole course: he hands the harmony of comparative advantage to every free-trader after him, and the conflict of the labor theory of value and class shares straight to Marx.

The modern counterpart — and what gets lost

"Ricardian" today

Two centuries on, Ricardo may be the most alive of all the classical economists, because his signature result is still taught in week one of every trade course.

What lines up

Comparative advantage is the foundation of modern trade theory and the intellectual backbone of globalization, the WTO, and every argument for free trade. The core insight — specialize by opportunity cost, not absolute skill — is simply, provably correct, and it applies to people as much as nations.

What gets lost

The model assumes labor can't move between countries, ignores the losers within a country when an industry collapses, and treats technology as fixed. "Everyone gains" is true for nations as wholes — not for the factory town whose plant moves overseas. Modern trade fights are mostly about that gap.

His theory of rent lives on too, generalized into the idea of economic rent — any income earned from owning a scarce, fixed resource rather than from producing anything. When economists today worry about landlords, monopolists, holders of patents, or owners of prime real estate capturing wealth they didn't create, they are speaking Ricardo's language. The gloomy stationary state is the one big call he got wrong, and for the same reason Malthus did: he could not foresee how far sustained technological progress would push back diminishing returns in farming.

The strongest case for him

Before you file him under "abstract"

Ricardo is sometimes dismissed as the man who made economics cold and mathematical — who replaced Smith's rich social world with a bloodless machine of three classes and a few equations. There is something to that. But it misses why the abstraction was a triumph.

Before Ricardo, economic arguments were mostly stories. Ricardo showed you could take a messy real fight — who should bear the cost of the Corn Laws — strip it to its logical skeleton, and prove a conclusion that no amount of storytelling could have reached: that protection enriched landlords at the direct expense of profits and growth, and that even a country superior at everything should still trade. Comparative advantage in particular is the kind of result that looks obvious only after someone has shown it to you, and before Ricardo, nobody had. Two hundred years of economists have tried to find the hole in it and failed. When a self-taught stockbroker reasons his way to a truth that survives two centuries of professional attack, "too abstract" is not the verdict — "this is what theory is for" is closer.
