Bentham, Say & Senior: the architects of harmony

Jeremy Bentham 1748–1832 · Jean-Baptiste Say 1767–1832 · Nassau Senior 1790–1864

Does market exchange harmonize our interests, or conceal a conflict between them? Ricardo left economics split — harmony between nations, open conflict over the harvest at home. These three writers pick up the harmony side and build it out. Bentham hands the science a way to measure happiness; Say argues the market can never seize up into a general crisis; Senior insists that profit is a fair reward, not something squeezed out of workers. Together they assemble the intellectual machinery for saying the free market serves everyone — and, our textbook warns, for quietly covering over the conflict Ricardo had exposed.

Three writers, one project

Who they are, in one line each

After Ricardo, economics branches. One branch — the labor-and-conflict line — runs toward Marx. The other — the utility-and-harmony line — runs through these three toward modern mainstream economics.

Jeremy Bentham
1748–1832 · London

The philosopher of utility. Morality and law should maximize the greatest happiness of the greatest number — and happiness, he insists, can in principle be counted.

Jean-Baptiste Say
1767–1832 · France

The great popularizer of Smith on the Continent, and author of Say's Law: production creates the income to buy what's produced, so a general glut is impossible.

Nassau Senior
1790–1864 · Oxford

One of the first professional, academic economists. Profit is the reward for abstinence — the capitalist's sacrifice in not consuming his capital.

They never worked as a team, and they disagreed on plenty. What unites them for our purposes is a shared drift: away from Ricardo's grim three-class tug-of-war and toward a picture in which the market, left alone, works for everyone. Our textbook (Hunt & Lautzenheiser) files all three under what it calls the utility tradition — and treats Say and Senior, in particular, as increasingly apologetic: writers whose main service was defending the emerging industrial order against its critics.

Bentham · the measuring rod

If happiness is the goal, can you count it?

Bentham's starting point is disarmingly simple, and it opens his most famous book:

"Nature has placed mankind under the governance of two sovereign masters, pain and pleasure."

— Bentham, Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation (1789), ch. 1

Everything we do, he argues, is aimed at getting pleasure or avoiding pain. So the right action — for a person, and above all for a government — is the one that produces the greatest net balance of pleasure over pain, added up across everyone it touches. This is the principle of utility, and its slogan is "the greatest happiness of the greatest number."

The radical move is the next one. Bentham thought happiness was not just the goal but a quantity — something you could, in principle, add and subtract like money. His felicific calculus (also called the hedonic calculus) lists the dimensions you'd weigh to measure the pleasure or pain from any act:

IntensityHow strong is the pleasure or pain?
DurationHow long does it last?
CertaintyHow likely is it actually to happen?
PropinquityHow soon will it arrive — now, or far off?
FecundityWill it breed more of the same feeling afterward?
PurityIs it free of a following opposite feeling (pleasure with no hangover)?
ExtentHow many people does it reach?

Notice what this does. It makes every person count equally — the duke's pleasure weighs no more than the beggar's — which made Bentham a genuine radical, a reformer of prisons, law, and the franchise. But it also treats pleasure as pleasure, whatever its source: he was happy to say that a simple game of push-pin, if it gives as much pleasure, is worth as much as poetry. Quantity is what matters, not the dignity of the source.

For economics, this is the seed of everything that follows. Once you accept that the goal of society is to maximize total utility, you have a yardstick. You can ask whether a tax, a tariff, or a factory raises or lowers the sum of human satisfaction — the exact question behind modern cost-benefit analysis and welfare economics. And it plants the idea that would detonate sixty years later in the hands of the marginalists (Build 6): that value itself comes not from labor, as Ricardo and Marx held, but from utility — from how much satisfaction the last little bit of a thing gives you. The game attached to this build puts you inside the calculus: you sit as Bentham's legislator and score real reforms across those seven dimensions.

Say · the market that can't seize up

Say's Law: "products are paid for with products"

Jean-Baptiste Say was the man who carried Adam Smith into French — clearly, enthusiastically, and with a sharper eye than Smith for the entrepreneur, the organizer who gathers land, labor, and capital and takes the risk of production. But he is remembered for one claim above all, the one that became classical economics' deepest article of faith.

The worry Say set out to answer was this: couldn't an economy simply produce more goods than people want to buy — a glut, with unsold stock piling up and workers idle everywhere at once? Say said no, and here is the logic:

"A product is no sooner created than it, from that instant, affords a market for other products to the full extent of its own value."

— Say, A Treatise on Political Economy (1803), bk. I, ch. 15

The point is that the act of producing something generates exactly the income needed to buy something else. When a shoemaker makes and sells shoes, the wages, rent, and profit paid out are the demand for other goods — the shoemaker now wants bread, cloth, tools. So supply, in the aggregate, carries its own demand along with it. A glut of one good is always matched by a shortage of another (you made too many shoes because you made too little of what people actually wanted). A general glut of everything at once — a demand shortfall across the whole economy — simply can't happen. Keynes would later boil the doctrine down to the famous shorthand "supply creates its own demand."

The implication is enormous and is the heart of why Say belongs on the harmony side: if the market can never suffer a general breakdown, then mass unemployment and depression are not built into capitalism. Any slump must be temporary and self-correcting, a mismatch that prices and wages will sort out on their own. There is no systemic flaw for government to fix — and no systemic flaw for a radical to attack.

This is exactly where Malthus (Build 1) broke ranks. Malthus argued that a general glut was possible — that the economy could get stuck with too little demand — and he and Ricardo argued it out for years. Ricardo sided with Say, and Say's Law became orthodoxy for over a century. Then the Great Depression made a general glut impossible to deny, and Keynes built his whole General Theory (1936) as a demolition of Say's Law. Say lost that round — but the question of whether markets self-correct is still the fault line in macroeconomics today.

Senior · profit as a fair reward

Abstinence — and the most famous bad hour in economics

Nassau Senior was something new: a professional economist. In 1825 he became the first holder of the Drummond Professorship of Political Economy at Oxford, and he wanted to turn the subject into a rigorous, value-free science built by deduction from a few obvious postulates. Two of his ideas matter for our question — one clever, one disastrous.

The clever one is the abstinence theory of profit. Ricardo and (soon) Marx treated the capitalist's profit as a deduction — a slice carved out of the value that labor produced, which raised the awkward suggestion that profit was taken from the worker. Senior reframed it completely. Profit, he said, is the reward for abstinence: the capitalist could have consumed his wealth, enjoyed it now, but instead he refrained, locking it up as machinery and factories so that production could happen. Interest and profit are the price of that sacrifice, that waiting.

"By the word Abstinence, we wish to express that agent, distinct from labour and the agency of nature … the conduct of a person who abstains from the unproductive use of what he can command."

— Senior, An Outline of the Science of Political Economy (1836)

See how neatly this dissolves the conflict. If the worker is paid for labor and the capitalist is paid for abstinence, then each is simply receiving the reward for his own distinct contribution. Nobody is exploiting anybody; the market is handing out fair wages for fair sacrifices. The harmony is restored — by relabeling the thing Ricardo called a deduction as a virtue.

The disastrous idea is the "last hour." In 1837, fighting the proposed Ten Hours Act that would cap the factory working day, Senior produced an argument that became a byword for motivated reasoning. He claimed that in a textile mill, with its heavy fixed investment, the entire net profit of the business was earned in the last hour of the working day — the earlier hours only covered costs. Cut the day from eleven hours to ten, he warned, and you would wipe out profit entirely and destroy the industry.

  • The claimAll profit comes from the final hour of labor, so even a one-hour reduction is ruinous. A rhetorical knockout against factory reform.
  • The errorSenior bungled the accounting — he ignored that raw materials and circulating capital turn over continuously through the day, not just at the end. Each hour contributes its share of profit.
  • The afterlifeMarx (Build 5) devoted gleeful pages of Capital to shredding "Senior's Last Hour," treating it as proof that the new economics had become a paid advocate for the factory owners.

The Ten Hours Act passed in 1847 anyway. The industry did not collapse. But the episode is why Hunt & Lautzenheiser hold Senior up as the clearest case of economics sliding from analysis into apology — theory bent to defend the interests of a particular class.

The system in one picture

How the three fit together

Line them up and you can see a single structure being built — a complete answer to the critics who would say the market serves the few at the expense of the many.

WriterThe one big ideaWhat it rules out
BenthamUtility can be measuredThe objection that we can't compare gains and losses across people — gives the market a scorecard it can "win."
SayNo general glutsThe objection that capitalism is prone to crisis and mass unemployment — makes slumps self-curing, not systemic.
SeniorProfit = reward for abstinenceThe objection that profit is taken from the worker — recasts it as fair pay for the capitalist's own sacrifice.

Bentham supplies the goal and the yardstick; Say promises the machine never breaks; Senior certifies that the shares are fair. Put together, you get a market that maximizes total happiness, runs without crisis, and distributes its rewards justly. That is the harmony answer in its mature form — and it is the target Marx will aim at in Build 5.

Where they land on the course question

Harmony — asserted, and partly to reassure

If Smith saw a natural harmony and Ricardo found a conflict hiding underneath it, these three are the counter-swing: a determined effort to re-establish harmony as the main story.

At their best, they were doing real science. Bentham's utility principle is the foundation of welfare economics and still the moral framework behind every cost-benefit study. Say's question — can demand fall short across a whole economy? — is one of the deepest in all of economics, and the argument is more subtle than its critics allow. Senior's instinct, that waiting and risk-bearing are genuine economic contributions that deserve a return, survives in modern theories of interest.

At their worst — and this is Hunt & Lautzenheiser's charge — the harmony was asserted because it was convenient. Say's Law told the factory system it could do no systemic wrong. Senior's abstinence and his last-hour arithmetic were deployed, in real political fights, to protect profits and resist a shorter working day. The harmony of interests started to look less like a discovery and more like a comforting story told to the people who were winning.

So Build 3's answer to our question is: harmony — but listen for the motive. These writers built the serious intellectual case that free markets serve the general good, and much of that case still stands. But they also show economics learning to reassure — to explain conflict away. That is precisely the move Marx will refuse to let them make, and the reason the course now swings back, hard, to the conflict side.

The modern counterpart — and what gets lost

You use all three without knowing it

What lines up

Bentham lives in every cost-benefit analysis and in the whole field of welfare economics. Say lives on in the belief — strong among many economists — that the economy tends back to full employment on its own. Senior's abstinence survives in the idea that interest is a reward for deferring consumption (the "time preference" behind every savings rate).

What gets lost

Utility turned out to be far harder to measure and compare between people than Bentham hoped — the whole thing nearly broke welfare economics in the 20th century. Say's Law could not survive the Great Depression. And "abstinence" rings hollow when the person abstaining is a millionaire choosing not to buy a third yacht, while the person laboring is choosing between rent and food.

The deepest modern echo is the one that runs through all three: the move from a labor theory of value to a utility theory of value. Ricardo and Marx said the worth of things comes from the work in them; Bentham's utility, carried forward by Say's and Senior's market optimism, pointed the other way — toward value as subjective satisfaction. That idea wins the argument in the 1870s with the marginalists, and it is still the foundation of the price theory you'd learn in intermediate micro today.

The strongest case for them

Before you file them under "apologists"

It is easy, following our textbook, to read these three as hired defenders of the factory owners — and the last-hour fiasco makes Senior an easy target. But that verdict is too cheap if it's the only one.

Bentham was a lifelong radical, not a reactionary: he fought for prison reform, animal welfare, the decriminalization of homosexuality, and votes for ordinary people, and his insistence that everyone's happiness counts equally was a weapon against privilege, not for it. Say genuinely believed free markets would lift the poor, and his law — whatever its flaws — forced economists to think hard about where demand actually comes from, a question Keynes could only answer by taking Say seriously enough to refute him in detail. Even Senior's abstinence theory contains a real insight that no serious economist now denies: that someone has to defer consumption for capital to exist, and that deferral is economically costly. The error was not in seeing that waiting matters; it was in pretending that a worker's forced sacrifice and a rich man's chosen one are the same kind of thing. Read them as thinkers wrestling honestly with hard questions — who sometimes let their conclusions be pulled by their sympathies — and they are far more interesting than any cartoon of the "market apologist."
