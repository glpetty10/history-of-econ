What each piece really means (say this in your breakout room)
The EngineBentham's felicific (hedonic) calculus: the claim that pleasure and pain can be measured and summed. Every reform's score is a sum of pleasures minus pains across the realm.
The seven dimensionsIntensity (how strong), duration (how long), certainty (how likely), propinquity (how soon), fecundity (does it breed more pleasure), purity (is it free of following pain), and extent (how many people). Each card's "why" lines tell you which ones moved the number.
Greatest HappinessThe total utils — the whole realm's net pleasure. Bentham's goal was to maximize this sum.
The Greatest NumberWhether more people were helped than hurt. Bentham's slogan welds the two together — but they can pull apart, and that is the game's real lesson.
The monopoly billThe trap. It can raise the total (a few merchants gain enormously) while wrecking the number (the many pay a little more). "Sacrifice the many for a bigger total?" is utilitarianism's most famous objection — you'll feel it directly.
Certainty & the factory actA nod to Nassau Senior: the mill-owners' threatened ruin is uncertain and overstated (his "last hour" error), so the calculus discounts that claimed pain.
Purity & the gin billA pleasure that looks huge but is followed by its opposite — ruin, the hangover — scores low on purity and fecundity, so a tempting "+" turns negative.
A learning model of Jeremy Bentham's felicific calculus (Principles of Morals and Legislation, 1789), with nods to Say's markets and Senior's factory arguments. The reforms and numbers are illustrative, not historical vote tallies — the calculus and its famous tension are the real point.  ·  Explainer · All builds
